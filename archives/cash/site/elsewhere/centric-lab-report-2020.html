Research report · 2020

Air Pollution and Health: Southall — a report for C.A.S.H. by Centric Lab

Centric Lab's pro bono study for CASH arguing that Southall residents are especially susceptible to air pollution and that PHE's population-level analysis could miss the harm. Summary and recommendations; the full report is available from Centric Lab.

This is a summary written for this archive, not the report itself. The full report is © Centric Lab and can be downloaded from Centric Lab’s research library. The date given is when CASH’s copy was saved (13 May 2020). The council’s 2020 counsel’s opinion also refers to the report as a 2020 document commissioned by CASH. Short quotations are marked.

What it is

A report “for C.A.S.H.” by Centric Lab, a London neuroscience lab working on urban health. Centric Lab says it carried out “a three month study” and was “instructed pro-bono to investigate if Southall Waterside, a real estate development led by Berkeley Group, had a role to play in the self reported spikes in health issues in the residents near the site.” It thanks Angela Fonso and Jo Griffin.

The argument

  • Susceptibility, not just exposure. Public Health England had judged that pollution from the site posed no health risk because levels were below guidelines and would disperse. Centric Lab argues that communities differ in how vulnerable they are to the same pollution. People under chronic stress, including the stress of deprivation, carry an “allostatic load” that makes them more susceptible.
  • Southall fits that profile. Southall Broadway is among the more deprived areas in Ealing and nationally (IMD 2019), and it has long been exposed to Heathrow and traffic pollution. The site added a further stressor on top of an existing burden. Residents’ awareness of the smell is itself a source of stress that can worsen health.
  • Small community survey. Centric Lab ran a 19-question survey. All ten scored participants rated as susceptible, partly because they live within 500 metres of the site. Of respondents, 81% had lived in Southall for over ten years and 90% reported disrupted sleep. The report acknowledges the sample is small.
  • Indoor soil vapour. Citing Loughborough University research, it argues that soil vapours can travel indoors and build up. Naphthalene measured at the soil hospital could therefore have accumulated in nearby homes.
  • Guidelines are a starting point. It quotes the WHO saying that for most pollutants no safe level has been found. It argues that decision-makers should set stricter, community-specific thresholds.

Critique of PHE’s approach

The report had not yet seen PHE’s fourth report. It warns that the GP and school-absence data PHE planned to use could hide harm:

  • averages can mask rising asthma among children and older people;
  • people on low incomes are less likely to report symptoms to a GP;
  • symptoms such as “viral-induced wheeze” may not be coded as asthma;
  • children often attend school while ill.

It notes that PHE measured chemicals at the site boundary, not near homes, and that dilution before pollution reaches residents “appears to be only an assumption.”

It proposes a cross-sectional study by PHE. This would look at susceptible groups (children, older people, people under chronic stress) and include indoor air testing in homes near the soil hospital.

Recommendations to the Southall Waterside developers

  1. Ensure all soil hospitals happen off-site.
  2. Slow extraction and give announced days of respite.
  3. Schedule heavy transport away from peak times and the school run.
  4. Publish a register of construction vehicle movements.
  5. Hire an independent air pollution consultant to advise on mitigation.
  6. Show on a public website that promised precautions have been implemented.
  7. Publish air pollution data daily on that website.
  8. Change construction methods on hot days.
  9. Use wind forecasts to plan mitigation and limit residents’ exposure.
  10. Investigate mesh window filters for nearby homes.
